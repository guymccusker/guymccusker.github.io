* COMMENT Denotational semantics and observations

** Operational semantics
Reduction relation:
\[
 M \rightarrow^{*} N
\]

Use this to define a notion of /observation/ \(\obs{M}\). Examples:
 - \(M \rightarrow^* 0\) in some typed language with constants
 - \(M \rightarrow^* \mathsf{hnf}\)  (aka \(M\) is /solvable/ in untyped \lambda-calculus)

From this we define a contextual preorder: \(M \lectx N\) means
\[
\text{for all contexts}~C[-]. \obs{C[M]} \subseteq \obs{C[N]}
\]

** Denotational semantics

Interpret (or perhaps "represent") programs as mathematical entities:

for a program phrase \(M\), its interpretation is \(\sem{M}\).

Typically \(\sem{M}\) is some kind of function
 - continuous functions between complete partial orders
 - homomorphisms of some algebraic structure

We'll mostly consider the case where \(\sem{M}\) is a /relation/.

Other kinds of models exist e.g. game semantics. 

** Denotational preorder

If the universe of interpretations carries a natural partial order, we obtain a /denotational/ preorder on programs:
\[
M \leden N \Leftrightarrow \sem{M} \sqsubseteq \sem{N}. 
\]

** Observations in denotational semantics

Typically, denotational models also allow us to make "observations": for example
 - denotational evaluation to values e.g. \(0 \in \sem{M}\)
 - denotational /definedness/: \(\sem{M} \not = \perp\)

We usually expect that the denotational partial order preserves observations i.e.
\[
 \sem{M} \sqsubseteq \sem{N} \Rightarrow \obs{\sem{M}} \subseteq \obs{\sem{N}}. 
\]

** Operational/denotational correspondence properties

*** Observational soundness

\[
 \obs{M} \subseteq \obs{\sem{M}}
\]

Typically easy to prove. It sometimes follows readily from  /reduction invariance/
\[
 M \rightarrow N \quad \Rightarrow \quad \sem{M} = \sem{N}. 
\]
plus simple facts about the semantics of normal forms. 

*** Computational adequacy

\[
\obs{\sem{M}} \subseteq \obs{M}. 
\]

This says that the operational semantics is /adequate/ to generate all observations predicted by the model.

*** Soundness

\[
 M \leden N \Rightarrow M \lectx N. 
\]

This follows from the observational soundness and adequacy properties, provided the denotational semantics is /compositional/ and /monotone/.

*** Completeness

\[
 M \lectx N \Rightarrow M \leden N 
\]

This property is notoriously difficult to establish for many kinds of programming language. For PCF this is the famous /full abstraction problem/.

In the \lambda-calculus, the situation is different.

** B\ouml{}hm preorder up to extensionality

** \(D_\infty\) models

In the standard domain-theoretic models of \lambda-calculus -- the so-called \(D_\infty\) models -- we have the correspondence
\[
 \sem{M} \not = \bot \Leftrightarrow M\rightarrow^*\mathsf{hnf}. 
\]

Taking a contextual preorder based on observing reduction to head normal form, we get
\[
 M \leden N \Rightarrow M \lectx N. 
\]

We also have
\[
 M \sqsubseteq_{\mathcal{B}\eta} N \Rightarrow M \leden N. 
\]

The "B\ouml{}hm-out" technique builds contexts that distinguish terms with distinct B\ouml{}hm-trees:
\[
 M \not \sqsubseteq_{\mathcal{B}\eta} N \Rightarrow \exists C[-]. C[M] \rightarrow^*\mathsf{hnf}~\text{but}~C[N]\not\rightarrow^*\mathsf{hnf}
\]

Therefore
\[
 \sem{M} \sqsubseteq \sem{N} \Leftrightarrow M \lectx N. 
\]

* Relational semantics

This talk is about the /relational semantics/ of the \lambda-calculus.

Relational semantics grew from Girard's /quantitative semantics/ and can be seen as a simple, degenerate model of linear logic.

** COMMENT A Cartesian closed category of relations

Our model will interpret programs as certain kinds of /relations/. They can be seen as morphisms of a cartesian closed category defined by:
 - objects: sets
 - morphisms \(A\rightarrow B\): relations from \(\mathbf{M}_f(A)\) to \(B\)

Here \(\mathbf{M}_f(A)\) is the set of /finite multisets/ drawn from \(A\).

#+reveal:split

Given relations 
\[
f : \mathbf{M}_f A \rightarrow B \quad\quad  g : \mathbf{M}_f B \rightarrow C
\]
we can compose like this: $g \circ f$ is 
\[ 
  \{ (S_1 \uplus \ldots \uplus S_n,c) \mid (S_i,b_i) \in f, ([b_1,\ldots,b_n],c) \in g \}
\]

If we can find a /reflexive object/ in this category then we have a model of the untyped \lambda-calculus.

However, the model is easier to explain in the form of a /non-idempotent intersection type/ system.

** Relational semantics as a type system

Idea: give a system of /type derivations/ yielding judgements of the form
\[
 x_1: \mu_1, \ldots, x_n:\mu_n \vdash M: \alpha
\]
The denotation of a term is the set of its valid typings.

In this case, each "type" \(\mu_i\) is a multiset of types, so we get something akin to the morphisms in the category described above.

** Intersection types

\begin{array}{rcll}
  \alpha  & ::=  & a \mid \mu \rightarrow \alpha \\
  \mu  & ::=  & [\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n] & n \geq 0\\
\end{array}

\[
\frac{}{x:[\alpha] \vdash x:\alpha}~\text{ax} \quad\quad
\frac{\Gamma_i \vdash M: \alpha_i~~(i \in I~\text{finite})}{\uplus_{i \in I}\Gamma_i \vdash M: [\alpha_i]_{i \in I}}~\text{many}
\]

\[
\frac{\Gamma, x:\mu \vdash M: \alpha}{\Gamma \vdash \lambda x.M: \mu \rightarrow \alpha}~\lambda
\]

\[
\frac{\Gamma \vdash M: \mu \rightarrow \alpha \quad \Delta \vdash N: \mu}
{\Gamma \uplus \Delta \vdash MN : \alpha}~\text{@}
\]

** Facts about the type system

- A term may have many types: the types are /elements of the denotational semantics/ rather than traditional types
- The @ and /many/ rules together implement the relational composition we defined above
- A term in head normal form trivially has a type. For example
  \[
   x: [] \rightarrow [] \rightarrow a \vdash x M N : \alpha
  \]
  Note that a term such as \( x \Omega\) has a type, though \(\Omega\) does not. 
- Subject reduction and expansion hold

** Subject reduction / expansion

If \( M \rightarrow_\beta N \) then
\[
 \Gamma \vdash M: \alpha \Leftrightarrow \Gamma \vdash N:\alpha
\]

Taking \(\sem{M}\) to be the set of typings of \(M\), this says:
\[
\text{if}~M\rightarrow_\beta N~\text{then}~\sem{M} = \sem{N}. 
\]

** Quantitative aspects

This model has /quantitative/ content: the semantic entities count something. But what?

The multisets keep track of how many times an argument is used: for example
\[
 \cfrac{\cfrac{\vdots}{f: [[\alpha] \rightarrow [\alpha'] \rightarrow \alpha] \vdash \lambda x. f~x~x:[\alpha, \alpha']\rightarrow \alpha}\quad \cfrac{\vdash M: \alpha \quad \vdash M: \alpha'}{\vdash M: [\alpha,\alpha']}}{\vdash (\lambda x. f~x~x) M: \alpha }~@
\]

The function uses \(M\) twice, so we need /two/ derivations of \(M\).

Though substitution may duplicate or discard /terms/, it does not duplicate or discard their /type derivations/! 

** Quantitative subject reduction


For /head reductions/ we can say more.

A head reduction is the contraction of a redex of the form
\[
 \lambda x_1 \ldots x_n. R~M_1 \ldots M_k 
\]

Write \(M \rightarrow_h N\) to say that \(M\) reduces by a head-reduction to \(N\).

Quantitative subject reduction: if \(M \rightarrow_h N\) and \(\Gamma \vdash M:\alpha\) with a typing derivation containing \(k\) instances of the @-rule, then \(\Gamma \vdash N:\alpha\) with a derivation containing \(k-1\) instances of the @-rule.

The restriction to head reductions is essential here: for example \(x \Omega \rightarrow_\beta x \Omega\), but this is never a head reduction. 

As a corollary:
a term \(M\) has a head normal form if and only if it has a type in this system.

In denotational terms:
\[
\sem{M} \not = \emptyset \Leftrightarrow M \rightarrow^* \mathsf{hnf}. 
\]
